Project Overview — Price Regression Full Pipeline

✓ Completed

One assignment, four price questions, one pipeline: can a regression model predict a price from an object's own specifications, and where does that model stop working? The module cards below are the implementation walkthrough behind this overview.

Research Questions

  • RQ1 — Car price: can we predict the price of a car (USD) from its technical specifications (engine size, horsepower, fuel type, body style)?
  • RQ2 — Laptop price: can we predict the price of a laptop (USD) from its hardware attributes (CPU frequency, screen size, RAM, storage, GPU)?
  • RQ3 — House price (King County, USD): which features (sqft living area, waterfront, floors) drive house prices, and can a regression pipeline predict them?
  • RQ4 — Medical insurance cost (USD): how do BMI, smoker status, age and other factors affect individual medical charges?

Approach

Every question runs through the same six stages — data import, cleaning, EDA, model development, cross-validation and tuning — with pandas, numpy, matplotlib, seaborn and scikit-learn in Jupyter. Cleaning converts "?" placeholders to missing values and applies drop, mean or mode imputation before dummy encoding (pd.get_dummies) and binning (pd.cut); EDA uses correlation matrices and heatmaps, scatter and box plots, pivot tables and Pearson coefficients; modelling moves from simple to multiple to polynomial regression (degrees 1–11) inside scikit-learn pipelines, scored with R-squared; validation adds train/test splits, k-fold cross-validation, cross_val_predict, Ridge regression and GridSearchCV over the alpha grid.

Key Findings

  • More polynomial degree is not more accuracy. On the Auto data a degree-5 model reaches training R-squared 0.557 while test R-squared falls to −29.87; 4-fold cross-validation returns 0.522 ± 0.291, and that spread is the instability itself.
  • Regularisation repairs an over-flexible model. Ridge with a GridSearchCV-tuned alpha (10,000 on Auto, 10 on Laptops) restores validation R-squared to 0.564 and 0.423, trading training fit for a test score that no longer collapses.
  • Feature coverage matters more than model family. King County house prices (USD) climb from R-squared 0.493 on sqft_living alone to 0.658 with multiple features and 0.751 through the full pipeline; insurance charges (USD) reach 0.845 with all features against 0.622 for smoker status alone.

Limitations

  • Course assignment: the models demonstrate the full pipeline rather than target production deployment.
  • Some datasets contain "?" placeholders that are imputed or dropped, so results depend on those preprocessing choices.
  • A few notebooks download their datasets from external URLs and need network access to run end to end.
  • No external or held-out temporal validation beyond the provided train/test splits.

Datasets: UCI Automobile (CC BY 4.0), a course-provided Laptops Pricing extract (IBM Skills Network), Kaggle King County House Sales (CC0 1.0) and Kaggle Medical Cost Personal / insurance (DbCL) — used for educational purposes only. Per-dataset source links and licence terms are listed in the project README.

Module 05 — Cross-Validation · Ridge · Grid Search

✓ Completed

Detect overfitting with k-fold cross-validation, stabilise high-degree polynomials with Ridge, and tune hyper-parameters via GridSearchCV. All price targets are in USD.

  • Auto — cross-validation: 4-fold CV mean R-squared 0.522 (±0.291). A spread of ±0.291 around a mean of 0.522 means the folds disagree strongly — the model is highly unstable, and a single train/test split would have hidden that.
  • Auto — the overfitting result: a degree-5 polynomial lifts training R-squared to 0.557 while test R-squared falls to −29.87. A negative R-squared means the model performs worse than simply predicting the mean, which is precisely the evidence of overfitting rather than a numerical bug.
  • Auto and Laptops — Ridge and tuning: Ridge with an alpha chosen by GridSearchCV (10,000 on Auto, 10 on Laptops) brings validation R-squared to 0.564 on Auto (train 0.859) and 0.423 on Laptops, where a single-feature CPU_frequency baseline scores CV R-squared 0.127 and 1–5 order polynomial test R-squared slides from 0.053 to −1.376.

Notebooks: Module05_Lab01_CrossValidation_Ridge_Auto.ipynb · Module05_Lab02_CrossValidation_Ridge_Laptops.ipynb

Data: module_5_auto.csv · laptops.csv (polynomial features)

Reflection

What this portfolio shows I can do, where the analysis still falls short, and what I would change next.

This portfolio records what I can now do end to end. I clean and explore data with Python and pandas, build regularised regressions in scikit-learn (Ridge tuned by GridSearchCV, Lasso from the same family) and validate them with k-fold cross-validation. I plot with matplotlib and seaborn, and my captions state conclusions, not axes. I keep the work in Git, publish it through GitHub Pages, and treat peer review as a to-do list: every PEER_FEEDBACK.md item is fixed or deferred with a reason.

The limits are equally visible. My degree-5 polynomial on the Auto data returns a test R-squared of −29.87, which says the features and sample size cannot support that flexibility. My alpha grid spans a single order of magnitude, so overfitting cannot be separated from an unlucky search range. Some datasets are course assets rather than openly licensed sources, and the split was made once rather than repeated, so my scores carry more uncertainty than the tables suggest.

Next I would swap the single split for repeated cross-validation, widen the alpha grid across several orders of magnitude, and do feature engineering before adding model complexity. Then I would fold Modules 01 to 06 into one reproducible pipeline and finish the profile material — CV and records — the review found missing.
